%----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\chapter{Evaluation methodology}
\label{chap:evaluation}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------

This chapter defines how the generated abstracts are evaluated. Every evaluation uses the 945 evaluation papers of \autoref{sec:splits} and the decoding settings of \autoref{sec:decoding-method}. Each generated abstract is compared with the paper's original abstract as recorded in the dataset; the cleaning described in \autoref{sec:cleaning} applies only to the training targets. The chapter first defines the compared configurations and then the measures. The measures address three questions in turn: how closely a generated abstract resembles the original abstract (text similarity), whether it distinguishes its paper from the other papers (identification), and which aspects of the paper's topic and research problem it recovers (content agreement). The results are reported in \autoref{chap:results}.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Compared configurations}
\label{sec:configurations}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------

I compare five configurations of the model (\autoref{tab:configurations}). They differ in the vector given to the prefix adapter and in the reader that turns this vector into an abstract. The names in the table are used throughout the following chapters.

The zero input, the reference mean, the GNN estimate and the oracle input all use the separately trained reader from phase~1 (\autoref{sec:training-reader}), so the differences between these four configurations come only from the vector. The joint model uses the reader after joint fine-tuning (\autoref{sec:training-joint}), which also updated the prefix adapter and the LoRA matrices. The comparison of the joint model with the GNN estimate therefore measures the effect of joint fine-tuning on the whole pipeline, not the effect of the input vector alone.

The main experiment is the sequence reference mean, GNN estimate, joint model. The reference mean is computed from the same references as the GNN estimate, but it weights them equally and has no learned parameters (\autoref{sec:gnn-estimator}). Comparing the two tests whether the trained GNN estimator improves generation over a parameter-free reference mean, and the joint model then shows what joint fine-tuning adds. The configurations also cover the two baselines named in the assignment: the zero input corresponds to generation without neighbourhood information, and the reference mean takes the place of a structure-agnostic encoder. Because the reference mean has no learned parameters, it is a simpler baseline than a trained structure-agnostic encoder would be; this deviation is discussed in \autoref{chap:discussion}.

The zero input and the oracle input serve as diagnostic controls rather than practical generation methods. The zero input removes all paper-specific conditioning. The zero vector is still passed through the trained prefix adapter, so the model retains a prefix, but that prefix contains no paper-specific input. The oracle input gives the reader the target's own embedding, computed from its title and abstract (\autoref{sec:embeddings}), which is never available in the actual task. Metrics are examined for whether they distinguish generation without paper-specific conditioning from generation conditioned on the target's own embedding; the reference mean provides an intermediate comparison. These roles are diagnostic rather than strict bounds: the oracle input supplies more direct information, but the reader is not guaranteed to exploit it fully.

\section{Text similarity}
\label{sec:text-similarity}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The first question is how closely a generated abstract resembles the original abstract. I first evaluated the generated abstracts with ROUGE-L and BERTScore, defined in \autoref{sec:overlap-metrics}, because each output has an original abstract against which it can be compared. ROUGE-L measures overlap in wording, while BERTScore uses contextual embeddings to recognise similarities beyond exact word matches~\cite{zhang2020bertscore}. Together, they provide an automatic starting point for assessing how closely the generated text resembles the original.

For both metrics, I report the F1 score, which combines precision and recall. ROUGE-L is computed with stemming, so that different forms of the same word count as a match. BERTScore uses the default English encoder of its implementation, RoBERTa-large, without importance weighting and without the optional rescaling, which does not change how configurations are ranked. Both scores are averaged over the 945 evaluation papers.

I first tested how the two metrics respond when generation is conditioned on randomly selected papers rather than the target's recorded references. For 200 of the 945 evaluation papers, drawn at random, a general-purpose language model without task-specific fine-tuning (\texttt{gpt-5.6-luna}, used through the OpenAI API) wrote two abstracts from the same prompt. The first was based on the titles and abstracts of the paper's recorded references, its real citations. The second was based on random citations: the same number of papers drawn at random from the paper's own arXiv category, excluding the paper and its references. Only the listed publications differed between the two prompts, and both abstracts were scored against the paper's original abstract (\autoref{tab:metric-check}).

    metric & real citations & random citations & difference \\
    \midrule
    ROUGE-L F1 & 0.153 & 0.112 & 0.041 \\
    BERTScore F1 & 0.841 & 0.822 & 0.019 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Mean scores of abstracts generated for 200 evaluation papers from the paper's real citations and from the same number of papers drawn at random from its own arXiv category, each compared with the paper's original abstract. Difference: mean per-paper difference.}
  \label{tab:metric-check}

Both metrics respond to replacing the real citations: the abstract generated from real citations scores higher for 94.5\% of papers under ROUGE-L and 97\% under BERTScore. However, these scores do not directly explain what content agrees with the original abstract. In particular, the high BERTScore values in both conditions should not be interpreted as evidence that both outputs accurately describe the target paper.

High scores do not guarantee agreement in the problem, method or finding expressed by the text. \autoref{tab:metric-pairs} illustrates this with pairs of short scientific statements that I constructed for this purpose. Replacing a single word reverses a finding, swapping two terms reverses the roles of two methods, and replacing the application changes the research problem; each of these pairs still reaches a ROUGE-L of at least 0.60 and a BERTScore of at least 0.95. A close paraphrase that keeps the meaning in largely different words scores lower on both metrics.

In the first pair, changing ``improves'' to ``reduces'' reverses the finding, yet the scores remain 0.93 and 0.99. The scores therefore cannot be read directly as measures of factual agreement.

These checks show that ROUGE-L and BERTScore respond to changes in the source information, but high scores can coexist with substantial differences in meaning. I therefore retain them as secondary measures and supplement them with two evaluations. Identification tests whether an output distinguishes its target from other papers, while a content-agreement evaluation assesses agreement in specified aspects of the paper's research context and objective.

\section{Identification}
\label{sec:identification}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Identification asks whether a generated abstract distinguishes its own paper from the other papers. A comparison with a single original abstract, as in \autoref{sec:text-similarity}, cannot answer this: a generated abstract may score well against its own original abstract and equally well against the original abstracts of many other papers. I therefore compare each generated abstract with all 945 original abstracts of the evaluation set and check whether its own paper's abstract is the most similar one.

Similarity is measured with term frequency--inverse document frequency (TF-IDF), which represents each text by its words and gives more weight to words that are frequent in the text but rare across the collection, so that words common to most abstracts contribute little. The vocabulary and inverse-document-frequency weights are fitted on the original abstracts only; generated abstracts are then represented using the same vocabulary and weighting scheme. I use sublinear term-frequency weighting and remove English stop words. For each generated abstract, the 945 original abstracts are ranked by cosine similarity, and the rank of its own paper's abstract is recorded. Ties receive the worst rank among the tied candidates. An empty output therefore ranks last, while an identical output for every paper cannot achieve high identification accuracy across the collection. I summarise these ranks in two ways. The top-1 accuracy is the share of papers whose own abstract ranks first. The mean reciprocal rank (MRR) is the average of $1/\text{rank}$ over the papers: an own abstract ranked first contributes 1, one ranked second contributes $1/2$, and one ranked tenth contributes $1/10$, so the MRR also gives partial credit when the own abstract is close to the top. Under a uniformly random ranking of the 945 candidates, the expected top-1 accuracy is approximately 0.11\% and the expected MRR is approximately 0.008.

I use identification for two purposes. It is one of the measures with which the configurations are compared in \autoref{chap:ablations}. The same identification procedure is also used on validation papers for checkpoint selection (\autoref{sec:training}) and in the diagnostic test used to select the decoding constraint (\autoref{sec:decoding-method}). Its limits follow from its design. It compares words, so a generated abstract that describes the right paper in different words can rank low. Its values also depend on this particular collection of 945 abstracts; they are not probabilities of identifying a paper among all published work. Finally, a high identification score shows that the output contains distinguishing vocabulary, but does not establish that its individual claims are correct.

\section{Content agreement and text quality}
\label{sec:judge-evaluation}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Text similarity and identification provide indirect evidence of content agreement, but neither explicitly assesses whether the generated abstract states the same research problem and task as the original. I therefore also use an LLM judge (\autoref{sec:llm-judge}), which reads both abstracts and scores their agreement against criteria specified in its prompt. The same judge also rates how well the generated abstract is written.

The judge is \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}, used through the OpenAI API with temperature 0. I use temperature 0 to minimise variation from token sampling. Identical requests can nevertheless produce different scores, so I measure repeatability below. The judge answers in a fixed JSON format, in two separate calls per generated abstract, because the two calls measure different things. The content call receives the original and generated abstracts and scores their agreement in subfield, research problem and task. The quality call receives only the generated abstract and assesses its coherence and language. Both prompts are given in full in \autoref{app:judge-prompt}.

\autoref{tab:judge-aspects} lists the five aspects. Each content aspect uses the same scale: 0 indicates different subfields, problems or tasks; 1 indicates related but clearly different ones; 2 indicates agreement with differences in detail; and 3 indicates agreement without such differences. The quality aspects are also scored from 0 to 3, with a description for each level in the prompt. I report all five aspects separately. The content scores assess agreement in research context and objective; they do not establish that the generated methods or findings are correct. The quality scores assess the writing without comparing it with the original abstract; a well-written output can still describe the wrong research.

In this experiment, mean content scores are close to 0 for abstracts generated from random citations and around 2 for those generated from real citations (\autoref{tab:judge-check}). These values correspond approximately to different research and agreement with differences in detail, respectively. The real-citation output receives a higher score for about 91\% of papers on each aspect. The generator of this experiment is a different model from the judge, so the judge does not score its own outputs.

To measure how consistent the judge is, I scored the same 100 generated abstracts three times with both calls (\autoref{tab:judge-repeat}). The abstracts were drawn at random from the outputs of the GNN estimate (\autoref{sec:configurations}). The mean score of each aspect differs by at most 0.08 between the three repeats, but the scores of individual papers vary: for the content aspects, the per-paper standard deviation averages 0.16--0.21. The repeat test shows that individual scores can vary even when the text is unchanged. I therefore interpret individual judgements cautiously and report paired aggregate comparisons with uncertainty estimates (\autoref{sec:comparing-configurations}). Repeatability on this sample does not establish that small differences between configurations are robust to repeated judging.

The repeat test assesses consistency under the tested settings, while the citation-control experiment assesses sensitivity to changed source information. Neither establishes agreement with human judgement. All scores come from one judge model. The scores are ordinal, so their means serve as descriptive summaries. In the 100-paper repeat test, mean coherence is already about 2.84 out of 3, suggesting that this aspect may have limited room to distinguish configurations.

\section{Reporting differences}
\label{sec:comparing-configurations}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------

All configurations are evaluated on the same 945 papers. I therefore compare them using paired differences: for each paper, I subtract one configuration's score from the other's and average the differences. This accounts for the fact that the same papers may be easier or harder for both configurations.

I report the mean difference $\bar d$ together with its standard error,
\[
  \operatorname{SE}(\bar d) = \frac{s_d}{\sqrt{945}},
\]
where $s_d$ is the sample standard deviation of the per-paper differences. This describes uncertainty across evaluation papers; it does not separately assess variation from retraining the models or repeating the judge calls.
