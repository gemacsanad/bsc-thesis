%----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\chapter{Training and results}
\label{chap:results}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------

This chapter reports how the model of \autoref{chap:method} was trained and how the resulting final model performs. \autoref{sec:results-reader}, \autoref{sec:results-gnn} and \autoref{sec:results-joint} follow the three training phases of \autoref{sec:training}: the reader, the GNN estimator and joint fine-tuning. \autoref{sec:results-final} evaluates the jointly fine-tuned final model with the measures of \autoref{chap:evaluation}; its comparison with the other configurations follows in \autoref{chap:ablations}.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Phase 1: the reader}
\label{sec:results-reader}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The first phase has to show that the reader learns to generate abstracts and to use its input vector, so that different vectors lead to abstracts of different papers. I follow three quantities during training: the next-token loss, the identification of generated texts on validation papers, and the gates of the prefix adapter.

The training loss falls quickly from 3.13 to about 2.2 nats per token within the first few hundred optimiser steps and then decreases more slowly, to about 2.10 by the end of training (\autoref{fig:reader-training}a). The validation loss is computed on the 806 validation papers from the oracle input, whereas training mixes own embeddings and reference means (\autoref{sec:training-reader}). It is also computed with the gates set to zero, which disables the paper-specific part of the prefix. At the first measurement, after 250 steps, the two validation losses differ by less than 0.001 nats per token. By the end of training, the validation loss has fallen to 2.128, while the loss with the gates set to zero has stayed almost unchanged at 2.213. At the end of training, disabling the paper-specific prefix therefore increases validation loss by 0.085 nats per token. This indicates that the trained reader uses information supplied through that branch.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{../diagrams/data/run11-reader-14541449/figures/reader_overview.pdf}
  \caption{Phase~1 training of the reader. (a)~Next-token loss on the training papers and on the 806 validation papers (oracle input), with the trained prefix and with the gates set to zero. (b)~Identification MRR on validation papers after each epoch; ring: selected epoch. (c)~Mean and maximum absolute value of the 80 prefix gates. Dotted vertical lines: epoch boundaries.}
  \label{fig:reader-training}
\end{figure}

Identification shows the same effect on generated text (\autoref{fig:reader-training}b). After each epoch, the reader writes a text for each paper of the fixed validation subset, once from the paper's own embedding (the oracle input) and once from its reference mean, and each text is ranked against the original abstracts of the same papers by TF-IDF similarity (\autoref{sec:training-reader}). Both inputs start at about three to four times the chance MRR of 0.043 and rise steadily. From the reference mean, the MRR increases from 0.126 after the first epoch to 0.530 after the fifth and stays at 0.528 after the sixth, so the fifth epoch is selected. From the oracle input, the MRR increases from 0.162 to 0.704. Identification from the oracle input continues to improve through the sixth epoch, while performance from the reference mean has approximately plateaued.

The gates of the prefix adapter (\autoref{sec:prefix-adapter}) are a diagnostic of the training itself (\autoref{fig:reader-training}c). They are initialised to zero, so at the start of training the prefix is identical for every paper. The mean absolute gate value increases from zero to approximately 0.10, showing that training moves away from the initial paper-independent prefix. Gate magnitude alone does not measure how much useful information the prefix contributes.

\section{Phase 2: the GNN estimator}
\label{sec:results-gnn}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The second phase decides which of the eight variants becomes the GNN estimator. The criterion is the mean cosine similarity between a variant's estimate and the paper's own embedding on the 806 validation papers (\autoref{sec:training-gnn}). The reference mean, which averages the same reference embeddings without learned parameters, is the baseline that the estimator has to improve on.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{../diagrams/figures/gnn_comparison/gnn_selection.pdf}
  \caption{Mean cosine similarity between the estimate and the paper's own embedding on the 806 validation papers, for the eight GNN variants over 30 epochs. Colour: layer type and depth; solid: cosine loss; dashed: cosine loss with InfoNCE. Rings mark each variant's best epoch; the black ring marks the selected estimator. Dotted: the reference mean.}
  \label{fig:gnn-selection}
\end{figure}

\autoref{fig:gnn-selection} shows the validation cosine of all variants. The selected estimator, a single GATv2 layer trained with the cosine loss alone, reaches 0.828 at its best epoch, the 28th, against 0.816 for the reference mean, an improvement of 0.012 in mean cosine similarity. At their best epochs, the other variants reach between 0.810 and 0.822, so all variants stay close to each other and to the reference mean; the two variants with two layers and InfoNCE stay below it. Across these eight training runs, one layer achieves a higher best validation cosine than two layers for both architectures and objectives. Adding InfoNCE lowers the best validation cosine for each architecture and depth.

The gain carries over to papers that were not used for selection. On the 945 evaluation papers, the selected estimator reaches a mean cosine of 0.833 against 0.823 for the reference mean. These results establish an improvement in embedding estimation; whether this produces better abstracts is examined in \autoref{chap:ablations}.

\section{Phase 3: joint fine-tuning}
\label{sec:results-joint}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The third phase connects the two separately trained parts (\autoref{sec:training-joint}). The reader from phase~1 was trained on own embeddings and reference means, never on GNN estimates; joint fine-tuning trains it on the input it receives in use. At the same time, the generation loss passes gradients through the prefix into the GNN estimator. \autoref{fig:joint-training} shows next-token loss, validation identification and the GNN's training cosine loss.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{../diagrams/data/run11-joint-14541449/figures/joint_overview.pdf}
  \caption{Phase~3, joint fine-tuning. (a)~Next-token loss on the training papers and on the 806 validation papers (GNN estimate), with the trained prefix and with the gates set to zero. (b)~Identification MRR on validation papers after each epoch; ring: selected epoch. (c)~Training cosine loss of the GNN, one minus the cosine similarity between the GNN estimate and the paper's own embedding on the training papers. Light curves: logged values; dark curves: moving averages over 100 optimiser steps. Dotted vertical lines: epoch boundaries.}
  \label{fig:joint-training}
\end{figure}

Training cross-entropy remains around 2.11 nats per token (\autoref{fig:joint-training}a). Validation cross-entropy, now measured using GNN estimates, changes little between the first and final measurements, from 2.152 to 2.149; because the input differs, these values are not directly comparable with those of phase~1. Disabling the paper-specific prefix increases validation loss by approximately 0.06 nats per token at both measurements, indicating that the reader continues to use this conditioning.

Identification shows how the reader adapts to its new input (\autoref{fig:joint-training}b). From the GNN estimate, the MRR rises from 0.592 after the first epoch to 0.616 after the second and 0.643 after the third, which is selected. Reference-mean MRR, an input the reader no longer receives in this phase, is lower at the end of the phase, at 0.484 compared with 0.525 after the first epoch. This suggests that the reader specialises in the GNN estimate. The mean absolute gate value also increases, from approximately 0.092 to 0.108.

The GNN's training cosine loss remains around 0.20, with no sustained decrease during joint fine-tuning (\autoref{fig:joint-training}c). Joint training therefore produces no clear improvement in this measure of embedding alignment. The identification results are consistent with the reader adapting to GNN-estimate inputs, although the loss curve alone does not establish how much the estimator's outputs changed. Whether the jointly fine-tuned model generates better abstracts than the GNN estimate with the separately trained reader is compared in \autoref{chap:ablations}.

\section{Evaluation of the final model}
\label{sec:results-final}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------

The final model is the jointly fine-tuned model of \autoref{sec:results-joint}. It generates one abstract for each of the 945 evaluation papers with the decoding settings of \autoref{sec:decoding-method}, and each abstract is scored with the measures of \autoref{chap:evaluation}. \autoref{tab:final-model} summarises the results. This section describes the final model on its own; its comparison with the other configurations follows in \autoref{chap:ablations}.

\begin{table}[!ht]
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llr@{}}
    \toprule
    measure & instrument & final model \\
    \midrule
    ROUGE-L F1 & text similarity & 0.176 \\
    BERTScore F1 & text similarity & 0.844 \\
    top-1 (chance 0.11\%) & identification & 18.8\% \\
    \midrule
    subfield & judge, content (0--3) & 1.30 \\
    problem & judge, content (0--3) & 1.36 \\
    task & judge, content (0--3) & 1.43 \\
    coherence & judge, quality (0--3) & 2.82 \\
    language & judge, quality (0--3) & 2.44 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Mean scores of the final model on the 945 evaluation papers. Text similarity compares each generated abstract with the paper's original abstract; identification ranks it against the 945 original abstracts; the judge scores each aspect from 0 to 3.}
  \label{tab:final-model}
\end{table}

Identification is far above chance: the original abstract of the paper is ranked first for 18.8\% of the generated abstracts, against 0.11\% under a uniformly random ranking. This suggests that the generated abstracts contain vocabulary specific to their target paper.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{../diagrams/figures/results/judge_distribution.pdf}
  \caption{Distribution of the judge's scores for the final model on the 945 evaluation papers. Each bar shows the share of papers at each score of one aspect, from 0 (lightest) to 3 (darkest); content aspects above, quality aspects below. The meaning of the scores is given in \autoref{tab:judge-aspects} and \autoref{app:judge-prompt}. Shares below 5\% are not labelled.}
  \label{fig:judge-distribution}
\end{figure}

\autoref{fig:judge-distribution} shows how the judge's scores are distributed. For each content aspect, approximately 43--45\% of the generated abstracts receive a score of at least 2, indicating agreement with the original abstract, possibly with differences in detail. Agreement is therefore partial: fewer than half of the generated abstracts reach this level on any individual content aspect. The judge scores indicate that related research content is frequently recovered, but agreement with the target's specific problem and task is inconsistent.

The judge rates the writing of the generated abstracts highly. For coherence, 84.0\% of the abstracts receive the highest score; for language, the scores divide almost evenly between 2 and 3. As noted in \autoref{sec:judge-evaluation}, coherence lies close to the top of the scale, so it separates the abstracts less than the content aspects do.

\autoref{app:examples} presents four original and generated abstract pairs selected to illustrate stronger content agreement and several limitations. Their interpretation and possible explanations are discussed in \autoref{chap:discussion}.
